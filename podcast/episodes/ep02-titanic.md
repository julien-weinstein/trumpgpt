# Episode 2: "The Titanic Sank Because of a Group Chat"

**Runtime target:** 38 minutes
**Thumbnail text:** HE SAID SHUT UP
**The B-Side angle:** The best-known Titanic stories are about the iceberg, the lifeboats and class. Very few people talk about the radio room. Wireless was a luxury novelty, run by a private company for paying passengers. That night, the two young men running it were swamped with passengers' personal messages, and the ice warnings kept getting lost in the pile.
**The bet:** Cal bets Nora she can't get through the whole episode without mentioning the 1997 movie: Jack, Rose or the door.

## THE B-SIDE: ACT ONE, "THE BOYS IN THE BOX"

`[MUSIC: ticking, building]`

**NORA:** Okay, strap in. I want to introduce you to the two most important people on the Titanic, and neither of them is the captain. They're Jack Phillips and Harold Bride, the ship's wireless operators.

**CAL:** Jack Phillips was the senior operator. He turned twenty-five *on board*. His birthday was April 11th, the day after they left Southampton. Harold Bride, the junior operator, was twenty-two.

**NORA:** Twenty-two and twenty-five, so basically two guys who'd be on TikTok today, running the most important communications equipment on the most famous ship in the world. And here's the twist. They didn't work for the Titanic.

**CAL:** Right. This is the thing almost nobody knows. Phillips and Bride didn't work for White Star. They worked for the Marconi Company.

**NORA:** As in Guglielmo Marconi, the radio guy.

**CAL:** The radio guy. In 1912, ship-to-shore wireless was new, and it was a business. The Marconi Company leased the equipment and the operators to shipping lines. And the main way that business made money was not safety. It was passengers.

**NORA:** Passengers paying to send messages.

**CAL:** "Marconigrams." First-class passengers could send telegrams from the middle of the ocean. On the Titanic it cost twelve shillings and sixpence for the first ten words and ninepence for each word after that. That was serious money at the time.

**NORA:** And what were they sending? Cal, I need you to understand I'm about to describe a group chat. They're sending things like "Greetings from mid-Atlantic." "Having a lovely time." "Meet me at the pier." "Book us a table." Hotel reservations, stock tips, gossip.

**CAL:** A lot of the passenger messages we know about are essentially social. People were sending messages because they *could*. It was the most glamorous new technology in the world. Imagine the first people on a plane with Wi-Fi.

**NORA:** It's the 1912 version of posting a selfie from first class. "Hashtag maiden voyage, hashtag blessed."

**CAL:** And the operators' job, the job they were *paid* for, was to get all of those messages sent. Safety messages, ice warnings, navigation updates were also part of the job. But commercially the customer was the passenger.

**NORA:** And then something goes wrong.

**CAL:** Late on Friday night, April 12th, the wireless set breaks down.

**NORA:** On a maiden voyage!

**CAL:** Phillips and Bride stay up most of the night, for hours, finding the fault and fixing it. And by the time it's working again…

**NORA:** …there's a backlog. Hundreds of rich people's group-chat messages piling up, and these two kids are buried. That's the setup. On the night of the 14th, they're exhausted and drowning in work, and, Cal, tell them what else happens.

**CAL:** That evening the Titanic comes within range of the big wireless station at Cape Race in Newfoundland. That's the first time they can send messages to North America in bulk. It's the moment the whole backlog can finally go out.

**NORA:** So on the night they hit the iceberg, the radio room is having its busiest evening of the whole voyage.

**CAL:** Yes. And all day, ships have been sending warnings about ice.

## THE B-SIDE: ACT TWO, "SIX WARNINGS"

**NORA:** Okay, let's count them. On April 14th, how many ice warnings did the Titanic receive?

**CAL:** The figure usually given is six. Different researchers count them a bit differently, but it's about six over the course of the day. And I want to be careful here, because this is where the "group chat" framing is a bit of an exaggeration.

**NORA:** It's *barely* an exaggeration.

**CAL:** Several of the warnings *did* reach the bridge. In the morning there's one from a ship called the *Caronia*, which Captain Edward Smith saw and posted for his officers. In the early afternoon there's one from the *Baltic*. Captain Smith reads it, and then he *gives it to J. Bruce Ismay*, the chairman of the White Star Line, who's on board.

**NORA:** And what does Bruce do with it?

**CAL:** Bruce puts it in his pocket.

**NORA:** He puts it in his POCKET.

**CAL:** And according to testimony, he later pulled it out and showed it to a couple of passengers at some point in the afternoon. It wasn't posted in the chart room until that evening.

**NORA:** So the CEO is passing round an ice warning like a fun fact at a cocktail party.

**CAL:** That's… broadly what the testimony suggests, yes.

**NORA:** So the captain knows there's ice somewhere ahead. But what about the warnings that never got to him?

**CAL:** Right. Around 1:45 in the afternoon a German liner, the *Amerika*, sends a warning about icebergs. It's addressed to the U.S. Hydrographic Office in Washington, so Titanic is just passing it along to Cape Race. As far as we know it never reaches the bridge.

**NORA:** Okay, and then?

**CAL:** Then, at about 9:40 p.m., a ship called the *Mesaba* sends a very specific warning. It reports heavy pack ice and large icebergs, and it gives coordinates. Coordinates that are directly in the Titanic's path.

**NORA:** And where does that one go?

**CAL:** It went nowhere. As far as we can tell, Phillips received it while working alone, set it aside, and kept working through the passengers' messages for Cape Race. It never reached the bridge.

**NORA:** It was *right in front of them*. The coordinates of the thing that was going to kill them, sitting on a spike in a radio room under a pile of "Arriving Wednesday, love Mabel."

**CAL:** We don't actually know there was a message from a Mabel.

**NORA:** There's always a Mabel.

**CAL:** And then, about 10:55 p.m., the *Californian*. It's maybe ten to twenty miles away, a distance that's still argued about. It's stopped for the night because of the ice. Its only wireless operator, Cyril Evans, who's twenty years old, taps out a message to the Titanic, informally, operator to operator. In the testimony it comes out as: *"Say, old man, we are stopped and surrounded by ice."*

**NORA:** And the *Californian* is so close that the signal is *loud*. It's basically screaming in Phillips' headphones, and it drowns out the faint signal from Cape Race he's trying to hear.

**CAL:** And Phillips taps back, *"Shut up, shut up, I am busy; I am working Cape Race."* `[CLIP]`

**NORA:** "I'm working Cape Race." And what was he working on for Cape Race?

**CAL:** Passenger messages.

**NORA:** The group chat! The Titanic said "shut up" to the iceberg warning because it was busy with the group chat!

**CAL:** Okay, I'm going to defend Jack Phillips here, because I think it matters.

**NORA:** Go.

**CAL:** By the conventions of the time, an informal operator-to-operator message without the proper prefix wasn't treated as an official navigation warning addressed to the captain. Phillips was exhausted and overworked. He'd been up most of Friday night fixing the set. Cape Race was faint, and the *Californian* was deafeningly loud right next to him. And frankly the Marconi Company's business model put him in that chair, sending passenger traffic for money.

**NORA:** No, I agree with you. I'm not blaming Jack. I'm blaming the business model.

**CAL:** And there's a whole chain of failures here. The ship was going fast, around twenty-two knots, which was normal practice for liners then, even near ice. The night was perfectly calm with no moon, so there were no waves breaking at the base of the iceberg to make it visible. The lookouts didn't have binoculars. And in the brochure version, the iceberg just appears out of nowhere.

**NORA:** But it didn't come out of nowhere. They'd been *told.*

**CAL:** They'd been told, and the information was in the ship. It just didn't make it the last thirty feet to the people steering.

**NORA:** And what happens on the *Californian*?

**CAL:** Cyril Evans, having been told to shut up, listens to Phillips working Cape Race for a while longer. Then, at about 11:30, he takes off his headphones, switches off his set and goes to bed. That's ten minutes before the collision.

**NORA:** Oh my God.

**CAL:** The *Californian* only had one operator, so there was no one on watch overnight. When the Titanic started sending distress calls a little after midnight, the closest ship in the area had its radio turned off.

**NORA:** And the *Californian*'s crew saw rockets, didn't they?

**CAL:** Its officers on deck saw a number of white rockets in the night, eight of them, and reported them to the captain, Stanley Lord. Nobody woke up Cyril Evans to switch on the radio and ask what was happening.

**NORA:** Okay. So, by the way, I would like a medal, because I want to talk about the "it was just a quiet night and they didn't know" version from a certain 1990s film, and I'm not going to say its name.

**CAL:** Careful.

**NORA:** I'm being so careful.

## CITATION NEEDED: "THE FIRST SOS"

`[SFX: typewriter ding]`

**CAL:** Citation Needed. Two myths today, because the Titanic is drowning in myths. Sorry. Badly chosen word.

**NORA:** Cal.

**CAL:** Myth number one: the Titanic was the first ship ever to send an SOS.

**NORA:** Aunt Deb sent me that.

**CAL:** Of course. The verdict is FALSE. In 1912 the traditional Marconi distress call was "CQD." "SOS" had been adopted internationally a few years before, and it had already been used at sea, by the American liner *Arapahoe* in 1909, for example. What *is* true is that Phillips started by sending CQD, and at some point Harold Bride suggested he try SOS. Bride later remembered joking that it was the new call, and this might be his last chance to send it.

**NORA:** Gallows humor. These kids were funny. I love them.

**CAL:** So the Titanic didn't invent the SOS, but it's the reason everybody knows it.

**NORA:** And myth number two?

**CAL:** "The Titanic was advertised as unsinkable." The verdict is IT'S COMPLICATED. White Star's own promotional materials hedged a lot. A trade magazine, *The Shipbuilder*, described Titanic and her sister ship *Olympic* as "practically unsinkable." Most of the "unsinkable" legend got amplified *after* the sinking, because it's such a perfect irony. The press loved it.

**NORA:** Ugh. We did that. Journalists did that. I'm sorry on behalf of my people.

**CAL:** And a bonus: the binoculars. Supposedly the lookouts had no binoculars because the key to the locker that held them was carried off by an officer, David Blair, who was taken off the voyage at the last minute. That one's IT'S COMPLICATED too. The key was real, and it sold at auction in 2007 for about £90,000. But whether that key was *the* thing that stopped the lookouts getting binoculars, and whether binoculars would even have helped on a moonless night, is genuinely debated.

**NORA:** A ninety-thousand-pound key. Somebody profited from that.

**CAL:** Hold that thought.

## WHO PROFITED? `[CLIP]`

**NORA:** Number four: whoever owned that binocular key, ninety thousand pounds. Somebody's great-grandkid made rent.

**CAL:** It was owned by the family of the officer for decades, and then a buyer paid for it. Allegedly.

**NORA:** Number three: newspapers. The Titanic was the biggest news story in the world, and the papers went feral. William Randolph Hearst's papers went after Bruce Ismay for surviving, a man who'd got into a lifeboat while women and children died. They called him "J. *Brute* Ismay."

**CAL:** Which is… harsh, but it's in the record.

**NORA:** Number two, and this is the one that makes me want to throw my cold brew: the Marconi Company and its stars. They told the operators to *hold their story* because it was "fixed for you so you will get big money." Then Harold Bride sold his exclusive account to the *New York Times* for a thousand dollars. And Marconi was right there with the reporters when they went aboard.

**CAL:** To be fair, Bride was a traumatised kid who'd lost his friend, had injured feet and needed the money. And his account is invaluable. It's one of our best sources.

**NORA:** I'm not mad at Harold. I'm mad at the *company*. And, by the way, Marconi stock went up after the sinking, because the whole world had just watched the wireless save seven hundred lives.

**CAL:** It's also true that wireless *did* save those lives. Without it, nobody would've known where to go.

**NORA:** And number one is safety regulation, which is the best kind of winner. Cal, tell them.

**CAL:** In the U.S., the Radio Act of 1912 required ships to keep radio watch around the clock. Then in 1914 came the first International Convention for the Safety of Life at Sea, which required enough lifeboats for everyone on board. So the next time there was a Cyril Evans, he wouldn't be the only one on watch, and the radio wouldn't be switched off while he slept.

**NORA:** No ship would ever be alone like that again. And it took fifteen hundred people dying for someone to make a rule saying somebody had to be listening.
