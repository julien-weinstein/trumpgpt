# Episode 1: "The Mona Lisa Was a Nobody Until a Handyman Stole It"

**Runtime target:** 35 minutes
**Thumbnail text:** SHE WAS NOBODY
**The B-Side angle:** The Mona Lisa was not the most famous painting in the world until 1911. A handyman stole it, the world spent two years staring at the empty wall where it had hung, and that made it a global superstar.
**The bet:** Nora bets that a world-famous artist was a suspect in the theft. Cal says it's an internet myth.

## THE BROCHURE VERSION

**NORA:** Cal, give us the brochure version. What did we learn in school?

**CAL:** The brochure version. Leonardo da Vinci paints the *Mona Lisa* in the early 1500s. Most historians think the sitter is Lisa Gherardini, the wife of a Florentine silk merchant named Francesco del Giocondo, which is why in Italian it's *La Gioconda*. Leonardo works on it for years. He never delivers it to the client. He carries it with him and fiddles with it until he dies.

**NORA:** Relatable. I have emails like that.

**CAL:** Late in life he moves to France at the invitation of King Francis I, and the painting goes with him. It ends up in the French royal collection. After the Revolution it ends up in the Louvre, where it hangs today behind bulletproof glass while millions of people a year…

**NORA:** …hold their phones up over each other's heads to photograph a painting that is honestly the size of a placemat.

**CAL:** It's about 77 by 53 centimetres. So yes, a large placemat. The brochure version says it's the most famous painting in the world because it's a masterpiece: the smile, the eyes that follow you round the room, the technique.

**NORA:** And all of that is true.

**CAL:** All of that is true.

**NORA:** It's just not why it's *famous*.

**CAL:** I'll allow the distinction.

## THE B-SIDE: ACT ONE, "THE INSIDE MAN"

`[MUSIC: heist-movie bass line]`

**NORA:** Okay. Okay okay okay. Let's set the scene. It's 1911 and the Mona Lisa is hanging in the Louvre in a room called the Salon Carré. Cal, how famous is she?

**CAL:** In the art world she's very well regarded. The Victorian critic Walter Pater wrote a famously overheated paragraph about her in 1869, and art critics adored her. Among the general public, though, she was one painting among many. She wasn't on mugs, she wasn't on T-shirts, and there were no queues to see her.

**NORA:** She was a working actress. She had *credits*. But she was not famous-famous.

**CAL:** She was a character actress.

**NORA:** She was a character actress! Thank you. Now meet our leading man, Vincenzo Peruggia. He's an Italian immigrant living in Paris, about thirty years old, and he's a house painter, a decorator, a handyman. And, this is the important part, he had *worked at the Louvre.*

**CAL:** He worked for a glazing contractor. The Louvre had decided to put its most important paintings behind protective glass, partly because someone had attacked a painting a few years earlier. Peruggia was one of the workers who built those glass cases.

**NORA:** So the man who stole the Mona Lisa had *personally built her security system.*

**CAL:** Yes, that's… not ideal.

**NORA:** It's like hiring a guy to install your burglar alarm and then he burgles you. Respect to the handyman, honestly.

**CAL:** I'm sorry?

**NORA:** Respect to the handyman. I'm saying it. He knew the building, he knew the staff routine, and he knew the frames. And he knew the most important fact of all: the Louvre was closed to the public on Mondays.

**CAL:** And Monday was the day they did maintenance and cleaning, so the building was full of workmen in white smocks.

**NORA:** So you can't spot one random guy in a white smock when the whole building is full of them.

**CAL:** The accounts differ a little here, Peruggia's own included. In the most common version he slipped in on Sunday and hid overnight in a storeroom. In his own later telling he walked in early Monday morning with the workmen. Either way, on the morning of Monday, August 21st, 1911, he's inside, and he's dressed like he belongs there.

**NORA:** He walks into the Salon Carré, and nobody's there. He lifts the Mona Lisa off the wall. She's painted on a wooden panel, poplar…

**CAL:** Poplar, yes.

**NORA:** …so she's heavy, about eighteen pounds, and with the frame and the glass box he'd *built himself* it's more like two hundred.

**CAL:** Well, actually…

`[SFX: DING]`

**NORA:** The bell! I got a bell for this exact purpose.

**CAL:** …the figures vary, but with the glass case and frame it was somewhere around a hundred and fifty to two hundred pounds. So you're in the right range. I just wanted to note that the range exists.

**NORA:** He carries it into a service stairwell, takes it out of the frame, ditches the frame and the glass on the stairs, and tucks the panel under his smock. Then he goes down the stairs, and the door at the bottom is *locked.*

**CAL:** This is my favourite part.

**NORA:** He's standing there holding the most valuable object in France and he can't get out. So what does he do? He unscrews the *doorknob.*

**CAL:** And it still doesn't open.

**NORA:** It still doesn't open! And then a plumber walks up.

**CAL:** A plumber employed by the Louvre, named Sauvet.

**NORA:** And the plumber sees this guy in a white smock struggling with a door, thinks he's a fellow workman and goes, "Here, let me get that for you." `[SFX: door creak]` He opens the door. He *holds the door* for the man stealing the Mona Lisa.

**CAL:** The plumber later told investigators about the workman he'd helped. He just didn't think anything of it at the time, because why would you?

**NORA:** Then Peruggia walks out into the street and gets on with his day. He took the Mona Lisa home on a Monday morning like he was picking up a baguette.

`[CLIP ENDS]`

**CAL:** And here's where it goes from embarrassing to genuinely astonishing. Nobody noticed it was gone for over twenty-four hours.

**NORA:** Tell them, tell them.

**CAL:** On Tuesday morning a painter named Louis Béroud comes in to sketch in the Salon Carré. He walks up to where the Mona Lisa hangs, and on the wall are four iron pegs and nothing else.

**NORA:** Just *pegs*.

**CAL:** He asks a guard where she is. The guard says she's probably with the photographers, because the museum *did* take paintings down to photograph them, so it wasn't a crazy answer. Béroud waits, and waits some more. Eventually he nags them into checking with the photographers.

**NORA:** And the photographers have not got her.

**CAL:** They do not.

**NORA:** And that's when someone at the Louvre realises they have just lost the Mona Lisa.

## THE B-SIDE: ACT TWO, "THE EMPTY WALL"

**NORA:** Okay, and now the real story starts. Forget the theft for a second. This is what happens *after.*

**CAL:** The Louvre closes for a week. The police search the whole building, hundreds of rooms. They find the frame and the glass in the stairwell, and they find nothing else. France basically closes its borders to look for it, and they search ships and trains.

**NORA:** And the newspapers lose their *minds.* Cal, I need you to understand this was my people. Tabloid reporters in 1911 had been handed the greatest story of their lives. It had a beautiful woman who'd gone missing, a crime nobody could solve, and some of the most embarrassing public officials in Europe.

**CAL:** The newspapers printed her face, again and again, all over the world: in France, in the United States, in South America.

**NORA:** And *that's* the moment. Before this, most people had never seen her. Now her face is on the front page of every newspaper on Earth. She's on postcards. There are songs about her and cabaret acts. People are making jokes about her.

**CAL:** And when the Louvre reopens, people line up, and they're not there to see the Mona Lisa. She isn't there. They come to look at the empty space where she used to hang.

**NORA:** They queue to look at four pegs! *More people came to see the gap than had come to see the painting.* `[CLIP]` It's the most famous blank wall in history. Imagine your ex is so famous that people pay to look at the spot on the couch where they used to sit.

## CITATION NEEDED: "THE SIX FAKE MONA LISAS"

`[SFX: typewriter ding]`

**CAL:** It's time for "Citation Needed," where I take a famous myth and give it a verdict: TRUE, FALSE or IT'S COMPLICATED.

**NORA:** Every true-crime podcast loves this one, by the way.

**CAL:** They do. Here's the myth. The whole theft was masterminded by a con man called the Marqués Eduardo de Valfierno. He hired a forger to make six perfect copies of the Mona Lisa. He sold each one to a rich American collector who believed he was buying the real stolen painting. And the real theft was just there to make the forgeries believable.

**NORA:** Which is a fantastic story. That's a movie.

**CAL:** It is a movie. It's the one with George Clooney.

**NORA:** It's not. Cal, it's never the one with George Clooney.

**CAL:** Here's the problem. That entire story comes from a single magazine article, written by a journalist named Karl Decker in 1932, more than twenty years after the theft. He said Valfierno told it to him in confidence and that he'd waited until Valfierno was dead to publish it.

**NORA:** Oh, that's convenient.

**CAL:** Very convenient. And no one has ever found a scrap of independent evidence. There's no forger, no buyers and no fakes. Historians have looked hard. There's no record that the Marqués even existed.

**NORA:** So Karl Decker basically wrote fan fiction.

**CAL:** My verdict is FALSE, or at least so unsupported it might as well be. It's a story that came along after a much more embarrassing truth.

**NORA:** Which is?

**CAL:** That the real thief was just one guy, and he kept it in his apartment for two years.

## THE B-SIDE: ACT THREE, "THE PAINTING IN THE TRUNK"

`[MUSIC: slow, melancholy accordion]`

**NORA:** Okay, so where *was* she? She's in a cheap room in Paris, in Peruggia's lodgings, in a trunk, and depending on which account you read, the trunk has a false bottom.

**CAL:** And the police came to his apartment. They were working through a list of everyone who'd worked at the Louvre. An inspector actually interviewed him, in his room. He was standing a few feet from the painting and didn't find it.

**NORA:** You cannot be serious.

**CAL:** And it gets worse. Peruggia had a police record, so the police had his fingerprints on file. And he'd left a thumbprint on the glass case.

**NORA:** NO.

**CAL:** In the version usually told, the French police filing system at the time, which was run by the famous criminologist Alphonse Bertillon, was organised mainly by body measurements, and fingerprints were an add-on. And supposedly the print he left was from his *left* thumb, while the files were catalogued by the *right.* Historians argue about the exact details, but the basic point holds. They had the evidence and couldn't match it.

**NORA:** They had his fingerprint! They had his fingerprint and his home address, and they'd stood in his house! This is the most incompetent investigation in the history of incompetence, and I covered the Knicks for two seasons.

**CAL:** That's a… niche reference.

**NORA:** It's a deep wound. So, two years pass. Then, in late 1913, an antique dealer in Florence called Alfredo Geri places an ad saying he wants to buy art. And he gets a letter, signed, I swear to God, "Leonardo."

**CAL:** Signed "Leonardo." He wasn't subtle.

**NORA:** No, he was not. The letter says, "I have the Mona Lisa. I want to bring her home to Italy." So Geri brings along the director of the Uffizi Gallery, Giovanni Poggi, and they meet "Leonardo" at a hotel in Florence. "Leonardo" takes them up to his room and opens a trunk. Underneath a pile of old shoes and shirts and junk…

**CAL:** …there she is.

**NORA:** There she is. And Poggi turns the panel over and checks the back, and it carries the Louvre's stamps and inventory marks. It's real. They tell Peruggia, very calmly, that they need to take it to the museum to examine it properly. And then they call the police.

**CAL:** He was arrested on December 11th, 1913.

**NORA:** And this is the part I actually care about. Why did he do it?

**CAL:** His story at trial was patriotism. He said Napoleon had looted the painting from Italy and he wanted to return it to its homeland.

**NORA:** And was that true?

**CAL:** Well, actu… no. No bell. It's not true. Napoleon looted *a lot* of Italian art. Loads. But not this. Leonardo brought the Mona Lisa to France *himself*, and it was acquired by the French king. It was French royal property for almost three hundred years before Napoleon was even born.

**NORA:** So he got the history wrong.

**CAL:** He got the history wrong, but he believed it. Or he said he did. There were also suggestions that he hoped for a reward, or that someone put him up to it. The truth is probably somewhere in between: a lonely, underpaid immigrant who was mocked by his French co-workers as "macaroni," who convinced himself he was a hero, and who also wouldn't have said no to some money.

**NORA:** And that's why I keep saying respect to the handyman. I'm not saying he was a good guy, he stole a painting. But this was a man who was treated like nothing by a whole city, and he walked into its most important building and took its most precious object out the front… well, out the side door.

**CAL:** Which a plumber held open for him.

**NORA:** And a lot of Italy loved him. He was a folk hero. People sent him gifts in jail.

**CAL:** His sentence was one year and fifteen days, and it was reduced on appeal to about seven months. By the time of the appeal he'd already served most of it.

**NORA:** Seven months, for the crime of the century.

**CAL:** And before the painting went home, Italy got to keep her for a little while. She was put on show in Florence at the Uffizi, then in Rome and Milan, and huge crowds turned out. Then she went back to Paris in January 1914, on a special train, with a lot of ceremony.

**NORA:** She came home a *superstar*. She left as a character actress and she came back as Beyoncé.

## THE FOOTNOTE

`[MUSIC: soft piano]`

**CAL:** I always end on what I call "The Footnote." It's one small detail that I can't stop thinking about.

**NORA:** This is the part where Cal makes me cry, and I want everyone to know I'm not going to cry.

**CAL:** After prison, Vincenzo Peruggia went back to normal life. He served in the Italian army in the First World War. Afterwards he went back to France, married, and had a daughter. He ran a little paint and hardware shop in the French Alps. He went back to being a decorator, an ordinary working man.

And he died in 1925 on his forty-fourth birthday, and almost nobody noticed. It was so quiet that for decades a lot of books gave the wrong year for his death. People confused him with someone else. The details of his ending were only properly established much later, by researchers going through local records.

So think about it. A man made a painting famous enough that billions of people would know that face, and the world didn't even get his own obituary right.

And whenever someone stands in front of the Mona Lisa and holds their phone up over the crowd, every one of those photos is partly his. It's partly the work of a handyman who built the box, then stole what was in it, and who also, I think, accidentally gave her to the whole world.

**NORA:** …*God*damnit, Cal.

**CAL:** You said you wouldn't cry.

**NORA:** I'm not crying, I'm *allergic to your footnotes.*
