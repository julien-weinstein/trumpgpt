## Episode 4: The Salem Witch Trials Were a Real-Estate War

- **Cold open:** "NEIGHBORS HANG NEIGHBORS! IS IT THE DEVIL, OR IS IT THE DEED TO THE NORTH PASTURE?"
- **The B-Side:** In *Salem Possessed* (1974), Paul Boyer and Stephen Nissenbaum mapped where the accusers and the accused lived. Most accusers came from the poorer, farming west side of Salem Village, which was aligned with the Putnam family. Many of the accused lived on the east side, nearer commercial Salem Town, or were linked to the rival Porter family. The trials sat on top of a long feud over land, church control and the Reverend Samuel Parris.
- **Act beats:** (1) the Putnam–Porter feud and the fight over Parris's salary and firewood; (2) the accusations of 1692 start, and Thomas Putnam's household files a huge share of the complaints; (3) Giles Corey refuses to enter a plea, and so his property can't be seized as easily. He is pressed to death under stones, and his reported last words are "More weight."
- **Citation Needed:** "Witches were burned at Salem" is FALSE. Nineteen people were hanged, Corey was pressed to death and several died in jail. Burning was a European practice. "Ergot poisoning caused it" (Linnda Caporael, 1976) is IT'S COMPLICATED, and mostly rejected by historians.
- **Who Profited?** Sheriff George Corwin, who seized the property of the accused; the Putnam faction; and the town of Salem's modern Halloween tourism industry, which Nora calls "a 330-year-long merch drop."
- **The bet:** Nora bets that someone convicted at Salem was not officially cleared until the 2020s. Nora wins.
- **The Footnote:** In 2022, Elizabeth Johnson Jr., the last convicted "witch" never to have been cleared, was formally exonerated by the Massachusetts legislature after a class of eighth-graders in North Andover researched her case and campaigned for it. It took 329 years and a school civics project. Earlier, in 1706, Ann Putnam Jr., one of the chief accusers, stood in church while her public apology was read aloud.
- **Tease:** "In 1932, the Australian army deployed machine guns against an enemy that could run at fifty kilometres an hour. Spoiler: the enemy had feathers."

## Episode 5: Australia Declared War on Birds. The Birds Won.

- **Cold open:** "MILITARY HUMILIATED! TWO MACHINE GUNS, TEN THOUSAND BULLETS, ZERO DIGNITY!"
- **The B-Side:** The Great Emu War of 1932 is told as a joke, but it's really about First World War veterans who were given marginal farmland in Western Australia under soldier settlement schemes. During the Depression, wheat prices collapsed, the government didn't deliver promised subsidies, and about 20,000 emus arrived to eat what was left. The veterans asked for machine guns because machine guns were what they knew.
- **Act beats:** (1) the soldier-settlers and the broken promises; (2) Major G. P. W. Meredith, the 7th Heavy Battery and two Lewis guns, with emus scattering into small groups "like guerrillas," and a gun mounted on a truck that couldn't keep up; (3) the withdrawal, the second attempt, and the disputed kill count, which ranges from Meredith's claimed 986 down to far fewer.
- **Citation Needed:** "Australia officially lost a war to emus" is IT'S COMPLICATED. It was a culling operation, not a declared war, and the press named it the "Emu War." Meredith's reported comparison of the emus to tanks, and his remark that they could "face machine guns with the invulnerability of tanks," is TRUE as reported.
- **Who Profited?** Newspaper cartoonists, the emus, and in the end the bounty system that replaced the guns and was far more lethal (Cal delivers this somberly).
- **The bet:** Cal bets the emus "technically" lost. Nora wins on the grounds that "the emus are still there, Cal."
- **The Footnote:** In parliament, a member reportedly asked whether medals would be awarded, and another answered that they should go to the emus, who had "won every round." Then Cal brings it back to the farmers, many of whom walked off their land within a few years.
- **Tease:** "She's the most hated woman of the French Revolution, famous for one sentence. She never said it, and the man who 'quoted' her wrote it down when she was nine years old."

## Episode 6: Marie Antoinette Never Said "Let Them Eat Cake." She Was Cancelled.

- **Cold open:** "QUEEN TELLS STARVING PEASANTS: 'EAT CAKE!' (SHE DIDN'T.)"
- **The B-Side:** Marie Antoinette was the target of the first mass-media hate campaign. Pornographic underground pamphlets, the *libelles*, invented affairs, orgies and crimes, and they were printed in large numbers. The pamphleteers included people who later blackmailed the crown. The Revolution needed a villain, and she was foreign, female and Austrian, which made her easy to cast.
- **Act beats:** (1) a fourteen-year-old Austrian girl is married off to a stranger as a diplomatic deal; (2) the Diamond Necklace Affair of 1785: con artist Jeanne de la Motte uses a sex worker dressed as the queen to swindle a cardinal. Marie Antoinette is the *victim*, but the public decides she's guilty anyway; (3) her trial, where she is falsely accused of incest with her own son, and her answer appealing to every mother in the room, which wins her sympathy even there.
- **Citation Needed:** "Let them eat cake" is FALSE. The line ("qu'ils mangent de la brioche") appears in Rousseau's *Confessions*, attributed to "a great princess," in a passage written in the 1760s when Marie Antoinette was a child in Vienna. Versions of the story were told about earlier royals.
- **Who Profited?** The pamphleteers and blackmailers, the revolutionary press, and Jeanne de la Motte, who escaped prison and published a "tell-all" memoir in London.
- **The bet:** Nora bets that the queen spent less on clothes than the king's brothers spent on gambling. Cal says it's unprovable. Nora wins on the strength of the documented size of the princes' debts. *(Writers: pin the numbers down before scripting.)*
- **The Footnote:** On the scaffold, her last recorded words were an apology to the executioner after she accidentally stepped on his foot: "Pardon me, sir. I did not do it on purpose."
- **Tease:** "Poisoned. Shot. Beaten. Thrown in a frozen river. And he was still breathing. That's the legend of Rasputin. The autopsy tells a much shorter story."

## Episode 7: Rasputin Didn't Survive Poison, Bullets and Drowning. A Prince Made It Up.

- **Cold open:** "MAD MONK REFUSES TO DIE! (OR: ONE PRINCE'S MEMOIR NEEDED A BETTER EDITOR.)"
- **The B-Side:** The superhuman legend of Rasputin's death comes mostly from accounts written by his murderers, especially Prince Felix Yusupov, who told the story for decades, including in memoirs he published while living in exile. A monster that wouldn't die made the killers into heroes. The autopsy and the forensic evidence point to a far simpler killing, probably ended by a shot to the forehead.
- **Act beats:** (1) a Siberian peasant mystic who seems to help the haemophiliac heir Alexei, and the Tsarina's devotion to him; (2) the night of 16 to 17 December 1916 in the Yusupov Palace basement, with cakes, wine and a gramophone playing "Yankee Doodle"; (3) the legend versus the autopsy, and the British intelligence theory that MI6 agent Oswald Rayner was present.
- **Citation Needed:** "The cyanide had no effect because he was supernatural" is FALSE, or at least there's no evidence of poison in the autopsy as reported. "A British agent fired the fatal shot" is IT'S COMPLICATED: it's possible, argued by some historians, and unproven.
- **Who Profited?** Yusupov, who spent his exile telling the story, and who won a famous libel case against MGM in 1934 over how a film depicted his wife (that case is why films carry the "any resemblance to real persons" disclaimer); the Bolsheviks, for whom Rasputin was proof the monarchy was rotten; and Boney M.
- **The bet:** Nora bets that a Hollywood studio was sued over Rasputin. Nora wins.
- **The Footnote:** Rasputin's daughter Maria fled Russia, ended up in the United States and worked in the circus as a lion tamer, billed under her famous father's name. She later worked as a riveter in a shipyard during the Second World War. She spent her life insisting her father had been a kind man.
- **Tease:** "In July 1518, one woman started dancing in the street in Strasbourg and couldn't stop. Then dozens of others joined her. And the city council's solution was to hire a band."

## Episode 8: The Dancing Plague: When a City Tried to Cure Dancing With More Dancing

- **Cold open:** "CITY DANCES ITSELF TO DEATH! COUNCIL RESPONDS BY BOOKING MUSICIANS!"
- **The B-Side:** The dancing plague is usually told as a medieval freak show. Historian John Waller argues it was a mass psychogenic illness in a city that had been broken by famine, disease and fear of a vengeful saint, St Vitus. The authorities' decision to build a stage and hire musicians was a reasonable medical judgement at the time, and it made the outbreak dramatically worse.
- **Act beats:** (1) Frau Troffea starts dancing in July 1518; (2) the crowd grows into the dozens, the physicians say it's "hot blood," and the council's prescription is more dancing, with hired musicians and a stage; (3) the U-turn: the council bans public music and dancing, and the dancers are taken to the shrine of St Vitus at Saverne.
- **Citation Needed:** "Hundreds danced to death" is IT'S COMPLICATED. The crowds probably grew into the hundreds, but the most dramatic death tolls come from later or poorly supported sources, and historians debate them. "Ergot again" is mostly FALSE. Waller argues ergot can't explain hours of coordinated movement.
- **Who Profited?** The musicians ("the only gig economy that's ever been literally killing it" — Nora); the St Vitus shrine; and the modern TikTok explainer industry (meta joke).
- **The bet:** Cal bets the council *didn't* really hire musicians. Nora wins.
- **The Footnote:** At the shrine the dancers were given small crosses and red shoes, anointed with holy oil and marked with the sign of the cross. For many it seems to have worked. What healed them was the belief that someone was finally taking their suffering seriously.
- **Tease:** "You've heard that the Dutch went bankrupt over tulips. It's the most famous financial bubble in history. But when a historian went into the archives to find all the ruined people, she couldn't find them."

## Episode 9: Tulip Mania Never Crashed the Economy. A Victorian Author Did.

- **Cold open:** "DUTCH GO BROKE FOR FLOWERS! HOUSES TRADED FOR ONE BULB! (CITATION: A BOOK FROM 1841.)"
- **The B-Side:** The popular version of Tulip Mania, with ruined families, sailors eating bulbs and people throwing themselves into canals, comes largely from Charles Mackay's 1841 bestseller *Extraordinary Popular Delusions and the Madness of Crowds*, which drew on moralising pamphlets from the 1630s. Historian Anne Goldgar's archival research (*Tulipmania*, 2007) found real price spikes and real broken contracts, but no evidence of widespread bankruptcy or economic collapse.
- **Act beats:** (1) the Dutch Golden Age, the "Semper Augustus," and futures trading in taverns; (2) February 1637: one auction in Haarlem fails to sell, and the prices collapse; (3) the morality pamphlets, Mackay's book, and how the story became the parable every financial journalist reaches for whenever there's a bubble.
- **Citation Needed:** "A sailor ate a bulb worth a fortune thinking it was an onion" comes from Mackay and is IT'S COMPLICATED, probably FALSE. "The Dutch economy collapsed" is FALSE.
- **Who Profited?** Charles Mackay and his publishers, whose book is still in print; every finance pundit since 1841; and the Dutch tulip industry, which still dominates the world market.
- **The bet:** Nora bets the most valuable tulips were *diseased*. Cal says "flowers can't be diseased, they're flowers." Nora wins. *(This is Cal's most out-of-character loss. Let him be genuinely stunned.)*
- **The Footnote:** The streaked, flame-patterned "broken" tulips that people paid fortunes for got their beauty from a virus spread by aphids. The virus also weakened the bulbs. The most beautiful tulips in history were dying.
- **Tease (season finale):** "Everyone knows the Library of Alexandria burned. Julius Caesar, an angry mob or a Muslim conqueror, depending on who's telling it. What if nobody burned it at all? What if it just… ran out of money? And Cal says he finally has a bet he can win."

## Episode 10 (Season Finale): Nobody Burned the Library of Alexandria. They Cut Its Budget.

- **Cold open:** "GREATEST LIBRARY IN HISTORY LOST IN BLAZE! (OVER ABOUT SIX HUNDRED YEARS.)"
- **The B-Side:** There was no single great fire. The Library declined over centuries through political purges, loss of royal patronage, war damage and neglect. Caesar's fire in 48 BC probably destroyed books near the harbour, possibly in warehouses, rather than the whole library. In 145 BC Ptolemy VIII drove out scholars. Aurelian's war in the 270s AD devastated the palace quarter. The Serapeum, the "daughter library," was destroyed in 391. The story that the Caliph Omar ordered the books burned appears centuries after the fact and is widely considered a fabrication.
- **Act beats:** (1) how the Ptolemies built the library, including confiscating books from ships in the harbour, copying them and *giving back the copies*; (2) the long slide: purges, defunding and wars, with no one moment; (3) why everyone wants the fire, because a fire gives you a villain, whereas neglect is a story about us.
- **Citation Needed:** "Caesar burned the Library" is IT'S COMPLICATED. "The Caliph ordered it burned" is FALSE, or at least deeply unsupported, since the sources are hundreds of years later. "Hypatia was murdered defending the Library" is FALSE: she was killed in 415, decades after the Serapeum fell, and her death wasn't tied to the library.
- **Who Profited?** Nora reverses it for the finale and asks **who paid**: the scholars, and everyone after them who never got to read what was lost.
- **The bet (season payoff):** Cal bets Nora that she can't name a single villain who "burned the Library" whose story holds up to the sources. Nora goes for Caesar and loses on the details. **Cal wins his first bet**, and **the "Nora Was Wrong" tally goes from zero to one.** Play it big, with confetti and a slow-motion recorder solo, and Nora graciously wears the bicorne hat.
- **The Footnote:** Cal closes the season by arguing that knowledge isn't usually lost in a dramatic fire. It goes quietly, when nobody pays for the copying, the cataloguing and the people. Then he gives a pitch to support your local library that fits the show's voice, and Nora cries, as is canon.
- **Season 2 tease:** Nora reveals Aunt Deb has sent "the big one": "Cal, she says the Great Wall of China is visible from space." Cal: "Oh no." Cut to black.

## Ideas for Season 2

Ranked by fame × twist:
- **The Great Wall is visible from space** (it isn't, and the myth predates spaceflight)
- **Columbus and the flat earth** (educated Europeans knew the Earth was round, and the "flat earth" story was popularised in the 19th century, including by Washington Irving)
- **Paul Revere's ride** (he was captured, and the poem that made him famous was written 85 years later)
- **Lizzie Borden** (acquitted, and the rhyme got the number of whacks wrong)
- **Cleopatra** (she was Macedonian Greek, the first of her dynasty to learn Egyptian, and lived closer in time to the moon landing than to the building of the Great Pyramid)
- **Vikings' horned helmets** (they're from 19th-century opera costume design)
- **The Boston Molasses Flood** (a 1919 tsunami of syrup, corporate negligence, and one of the first big class-action cases)
- **Roswell** (the "weather balloon" was a cover story, though for a classified nuclear-test detection programme, Project Mogul, not aliens)
- **Watergate** (the deepest twist is how ordinary the break-in was, and that a security guard named Frank Wills found the tape on a door. Respect to the handyman.)
- **"Lost" Roanoke Colony** (CROATOAN, and new archaeology)
