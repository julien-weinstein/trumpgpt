# Footnotes & Felonies: Topic Selection and Audience Growth Plan

## How we pick events

An event makes the show only if it scores well on all four tests.

| Test | Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Fame** | Would a random person on the street recognise the name in five seconds? | Search volume and recognition in the browse feed. People click on things they already know. |
| **Twist** | Is there a true, sourced angle that turns the common version upside down? | The title has to promise "you've been told this wrong." That promise is the click. |
| **Human** | Is there an ordinary person at the centre we can root for or be horrified by? | This drives retention. Listeners stay for people, not dates. |
| **Comedy** | Is there real absurdity in the record? | This is our edge over the serious history shows. |

Avoid topics where the comedy would punch down, such as genocides or recent mass-casualty events. Their dark history isn't off-limits, but it doesn't suit this show's format.

## Why Season 1 is ordered this way

- **Episode 1 has to be the most shareable title we have.** "The Mona Lisa Was a Nobody Until a Handyman Stole It" is a provocative claim, but a true one, about the most famous painting on Earth. It also sets up the "Respect to the Handyman" bit.
- **Episode 2 is the subject with the most search volume.** The Titanic is a permanent top-tier history search. If listeners find Episode 2 first, it sends them back to Episode 1.
- **Episode 3 is the most meme-able topic.** Napoleon's height is a debate everyone already has an opinion on, which drives comments.
- **Episodes 4 to 9 alternate** between serious famous events (Salem, Rasputin, Marie Antoinette) and absurd famous events (the Emu War, the Dancing Plague, Tulip Mania). That keeps the feed from tiring listeners out.
- **Episode 10, the finale, is a two-hander.** Cal finally wins a bet, which pays off the tally gag.

## Titles, thumbnails and hooks

**Title formula:** `[Famous Thing] + [Contradiction or Accusation]`
- ✅ "The Titanic Sank Because of a Group Chat"
- ✅ "Napoleon Was Tall. A British Cartoonist Ruined His Life."
- ❌ "The Titanic: A New Perspective" (too vague, nobody clicks it)

Titles must be **defensible**. Each one is an exaggeration that the episode then earns and justifies on air. Never use a claim the episode later admits is false, because audiences punish clickbait that doesn't pay off.

**Thumbnail text:** three to five words, never the same as the title.
- Mona Lisa: "SHE WAS NOBODY"
- Titanic: "HE SAID SHUT UP"
- Napoleon: "5'7" ACTUALLY"

**The first 30 seconds decide retention.** Every cold open:
1. Starts mid-scene, with no "welcome to the show."
2. States the twist as a claim in the first 15 seconds.
3. Ends on a question the episode answers.

## Distribution

| Channel | What goes there |
|---|---|
| Audio (Spotify, Apple, everywhere) | The full episode with chapters that match the beat sheet |
| YouTube | Full video episode (both hosts on camera, with B-roll of primary sources on screen when Cal cites them) |
| Shorts, Reels, TikTok | Three to five `[CLIP]` moments per episode, the cold open always among them, captioned and vertical |
| Newsletter | "The Footnote" in written form plus the source list, which drives return listens |

**Cadence:** weekly, on the same day and at the same hour (Tuesday, 5:00 a.m. Eastern, so it's in the queue for commutes). Launch with **Episodes 1 to 3 at once** so new listeners can binge.

## Built-in engagement mechanics

- **The Bet**: listeners guess the outcome in the comments before the reveal.
- **Ding count**: the number of Well-Actually Bell rings goes in the description, and fans keep score.
- **"Who Profited?"**: each ranked list invites people to disagree ("you put Picasso too low").
- **Aunt Deb mailbag**: listeners send in the history memes their own relatives share, and the best ones become episode openers.
- **The end tease**: always a cliffhanger question about the next episode, never a plain topic name.
